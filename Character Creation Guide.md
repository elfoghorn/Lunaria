# Character Creation Guide

Building a character for Lunaria isn't just about picking a class and race — it's about answering one question: **what does your character want, and what does the world owe them?**

This guide walks you through building a backstory that feels real, connects to the world, and gives your DM threads to pull on.

---

## The Four Questions

Every good backstory answers four things. You don't need paragraphs — a sentence each is enough to start.

### 1. Where are you from?
Not just the kingdom — the *texture* of it. A Kairan merchant's kid from Nassem's Seat grew up with forty languages on the street and the best food in the world. A Fringe-born human from the Deepward grew up watching the Threshold Line through the floorboards. Same world, completely different people.

Ask yourself: what did your childhood smell like? What did adults in your community worry about? What was normal to you that would seem strange to someone from a different kingdom?

### 2. What do you want?
Not your class goal — your *personal* want. Something specific and a little vulnerable.

Vague wants make weak characters:
> *"I want to be a great warrior."*

Specific wants make interesting ones:
> *"I want to find out what really happened to my mother's trade route before the wind-lanes failed."*
> *"I want to prove to the Verath Senate that the document they sealed is a lie."*
> *"I want enough money to buy my family out of Lowergate before the next Threshold measurement comes out."*

The more specific the want, the more the world has something to push back against.

### 3. What do you believe that might be wrong?
The best characters carry a conviction that the story will test. It doesn't have to be a flaw — it's a worldview that made sense where you came from that might crack when you see more.

> *"I believe the Ascendant Church genuinely elevated us. I was raised on it. I've never had a reason to doubt it."*

> *"I think the Compact is hiding something about the lift-stone supply. I don't have proof. I've been right about things like this before."*

> *"I don't trust the Verdant Reach. I was taught that dragon-bonded people put the bond before everything else, and I've seen nothing to change that."*

### 4. What do you owe, and to whom?
Obligations are the engine of character arcs. Debts, loyalties, promises, family. Something that can complicate a decision at exactly the wrong moment.

> *"I owe the Seekers' Concord three more runs before my contract expires. I can't walk away yet."*

> *"My younger brother is still in the Ember Quarter. He believes in what they're building. I'm not sure I do anymore."*

## Structuring Your Backstory

**Keep it short.** A paragraph or two is better than a page. The DM needs hooks, not a novel. Leave space for the story to fill in.

A simple structure that works:

```
[Where you came from] shaped you because [specific thing].
You left / you stayed because [inciting moment or ongoing situation].
You want [specific thing] because [personal reason].
You're carrying [obligation or secret] that you haven't resolved.
```

**Don't write your backstory as a complete story.** Write it as setup. The interesting part hasn't happened yet — that's the campaign.

## What Makes a Backstory Land in Lunaria

### Connect to something real in the world
The Calamity was one hundred years ago. Everyone has grandparents' stories about it. The Below is rising. The wind-lanes are failing. The succession in Aethon is unresolved. These aren't background details — they're the things your character grew up hearing about, arguing about, being afraid of.

A character with no opinion on the Calamity is like a character with no opinion on the city they grew up in. Give yourself a position, even a quiet one.

### Use the social texture of your kingdom
Each kingdom has a cultural logic. Lean into it:

- **Aethoni** grew up with the Church in their daily life. They have a relationship with its theology — devout, quietly rebellious, rote and hollow, or genuinely wrestling with it.
- **Verathi** were raised on debate. They have opinions and they argue for them with evidence.
- **Kairan** characters know that commerce is diplomacy and hospitality is strategy. They read rooms.
- **Compact-born** carry their ancestors with them as practice, not metaphor. They are patient. They do not hurry.
- **Myren characters** know which tier they're from because it shapes everything about how people treat them.
- **Republic citizens** have an opinion on the founding. The Night of Chains is living history, not distant myth.
- **Reach-born** live in the present moment. The question is always *what is needed now*, not *what is the strategy*.

### Leave a question unanswered
The most effective backstory element is one that doesn't have a resolution yet. Not *"my father died and I avenged him"* but *"my father disappeared and I still don't know why."*

Unresolved things give your DM material. Resolved things are history.

## Example Backstories

These are sketches, not finished backstories — they show the shape of one, not every detail.

---

### The Ashwalker Seeker
*A human with Ashwalker heritage, raised near the Deepward Fringe, working as a Seekers' Concord Runner.*

> Jessa grew up in a Fringe community three hundred metres above the Threshold Line, close enough that on still mornings the ash-smell came up through the ventilation. Her fragment-sense — the warmth that means corruption is near — was treated as normal by her family and quietly unnerving to everyone who visited from above. She joined the Seekers at nineteen for the contract pay, stayed because the work made her useful in a way that nothing above the Fringe had. She wants to find the source of the fragment-warm spot her team marked in Year 96 and then never went back to. She owes the Concord two more standard runs before she can take unsanctioned work. She believes the Below is manageable if you respect it, which she knows is not a popular position and has stopped arguing about.

**What makes this work:** specific want, specific debt, a belief that the world will test, and a character shaped by where she's from rather than just *what she can do*.

---

### The Verathi Scholar Out of Her Depth
*A high elf archivist who has spent two centuries in the Grand Archive and is now somewhere she can't cross-reference.*

> Aelindra has worked in the Grand Archive for two hundred and three years. She has never been wrong about a primary source document. Eight weeks ago she found a document that contradicts something fundamental about the official history of Year 0 — not a detail, a load-bearing fact — and the request to bring it before the Archive Council was deferred without explanation. She has since been given a sabbatical she didn't request and a research appointment in a city three days from Verath-Ul. She wants to understand why the document was suppressed rather than challenged. She trusts institutional process in the way that only someone who has watched it work for two centuries can, which means she is going to have to be shown — not told — that it has failed.

**What makes this work:** the backstory sets up a specific present-day problem. She's not here because of something that happened to her parents. She's here because of something that happened last month.

---

### The Republic Citizen Carrying the Wall
*A tiefling who grew up in Freehold with a name on the Wall of Names.*

> Soren's grandfather is on the Wall of Names. Not in the section for people who died in the Night of Chains — in the section for people who compromised. His grandfather made a deal with the Aethon administration that saved forty people and surrendered twelve others to colonial custody. Soren has read the accounting a hundred times. He still doesn't know how to carry it. He works as a courier through the Ashkin Republic and occasionally into Aethon territory, which people find surprising and he finds necessary. He wants to understand what his grandfather knew that isn't in the record. He believes the Republic's founding principles are correct and is watching the Ember faction with increasing concern, because people who are certain the principles give them permission to do difficult things are the same kind of people his grandfather was, and he knows how that ends.

**What makes this work:** moral complexity without a clean answer. The backstory is a question the character can't resolve by themselves — which makes it a story.

---

### The Kairan Merchant Watching the Wind-Lanes
*A human trader whose entire operation depends on infrastructure that's quietly failing.*

> Davan built a three-route trading operation over eight years out of the Grand Bazaar's eastern expansion. He has good relationships with two djinn wind-lane operators, a reliable crew, and a commercial reputation that took seven years to build. For eighteen months the lane timing has been off — small at first, then measurably, then significantly. The Sultanate says it is being addressed. Davan's competitors are not talking about it publicly because none of them want to trigger a market panic. He wants to know whether the wind-lanes are going to recover, and if not, how much time he has before his competitive advantage becomes a liability. He's been offered a contract to transport something he can't verify for a buyer he can't identify. The price would cover six months of losses. He has not said yes. He has not said no.

**What makes this work:** a real-world problem with stakes. The character doesn't need a tragic past to have something to lose.

## A Note on Tragedy

Not every backstory needs a dead parent, a burned village, or a lost love.

Trauma is real and it's a legitimate character engine. But it's not the only one. Characters can be driven by ambition, curiosity, loyalty, debt, principle, grief for living people, or just a really specific question they need answered.

If you do include tragedy, make sure it's doing work. Ask: *how does this shape what my character wants right now?* If the answer is vague ("it made me stronger"), consider what specific *specific* thing it made your character want, fear, or believe.
